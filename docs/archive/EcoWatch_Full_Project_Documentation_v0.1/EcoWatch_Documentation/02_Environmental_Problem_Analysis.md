# 02 — Environmental Problem Analysis

**Status:** Problem framing; site-specific evidence and expert review are still required.

## 1. Purpose

This document explains why EcoWatch should analyse environmental change as a connected system and what evidence is needed to avoid unsupported conclusions.

## 2. Environmental pressures and effects

Environmental degradation can arise from multiple pressures, including land disturbance, mining, deforestation, waste disposal, agricultural runoff, erosion, infrastructure development and flooding. The presence and importance of each pressure varies by location.

A possible chain of effects may include:
- Land disturbance or vegetation removal.
- Increased erosion or movement of sediment.
- Changes in nearby surface-water quality.
- Effects on aquatic habitats and organisms.
- Effects on water used for farming, household purposes or livelihoods.
- Economic and social consequences for affected communities.

This is an analytical framework, not a claim that every link occurs in every location. The actual pathway depends on local geology, hydrology, contaminants, rainfall, land use, exposure and other conditions.

## 3. Why separate monitoring can be insufficient

A land-cover map may show disturbance but not establish its cause. A water-quality measurement may show a change but not identify the source. A community report may describe an impact but lack exact location or measurement details. A restoration report may show that work occurred without demonstrating recovery.

EcoWatch should allow these records to be connected for investigation while retaining their individual limitations.

## 4. Baseline and change history

A baseline describes environmental conditions during a defined reference period. It must not automatically be equated with the oldest image available.

For each monitored area:
1. Identify available historical satellite images, measurements, maps, reports and local knowledge.
2. Determine the dates and seasons represented.
3. Identify when the earliest reliable signs of change appear.
4. Compare multiple time points rather than relying only on a before-and-after pair.
5. Where possible, compare with a suitable reference area that has not experienced the same suspected pressure.
6. Document missing periods and limitations.

### Baseline quality categories
- **Directly measured:** a measurement taken and documented at the time.
- **Observed in source imagery:** a visible feature interpreted from an image.
- **Reconstructed:** an estimate derived from available historical evidence.
- **Predicted:** a model output about a possible condition.
- **Reported:** a statement supplied by a person or organisation.

These categories must not be blended. A model reconstruction must not be displayed as if it were a historical field measurement.

## 5. Seasonality and natural variation

Environmental indicators may vary naturally. Rainfall can affect turbidity, river flow and vegetation appearance. Agricultural cycles and seasonal vegetation changes may resemble degradation in some images.

Comparisons should therefore consider:
- Season and rainfall conditions.
- Sensor and image quality.
- Cloud cover and image resolution.
- Differences in measurement method.
- Normal variation in the indicator.
- Changes in the reference area.

A change between two images is not automatically evidence of illegal activity or pollution.

## 6. Potential indicator groups

| Domain | Possible indicators | Important limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Land | Land-cover change, bare soil, disturbed area, erosion features | Imagery may not identify cause or legal status |
| Water | Turbidity, pH, conductivity, dissolved oxygen, selected contaminants, flow | Requires suitable sampling methods and quality control |
| Vegetation | Vegetation cover, fragmentation, restoration growth | Seasonal changes and image resolution matter |
| Biodiversity | Species observations, habitat condition, mortality reports | Requires expert methods and verified observations |
| Community and livelihoods | Reported access, farming/fishing effects, service disruption | Sensitive, contextual and not interchangeable with ecological measurements |
| Climate and weather | Rainfall, temperature, drought or flood context | Can explain variation but does not alone establish a pollution source |

The pilot must choose a small set of indicators that can realistically be sourced, interpreted and verified.

## 7. Evidence needed to investigate a suspected relationship

For a suspected land-to-water impact, relevant evidence may include:
- Location and extent of land disturbance.
- Drainage paths and watershed boundaries.
- Rainfall and flow conditions.
- Upstream and downstream water measurements.
- Sampling dates, locations and laboratory methods.
- Other possible pollution sources.
- Historical records and field observations.

A spatial or temporal association is a reason to investigate, not proof of causation.

## 8. Environmental and community context

Environmental records should not reduce people to data points. EcoWatch should consider how environmental conditions affect access to water, farming, fishing, health-related concerns and livelihoods, while avoiding unsupported medical conclusions.

Community-reported impacts should be clearly identified as reports unless independently measured or assessed.

## 9. Pilot problem statement

The pilot problem statement should be finalised after selecting a location and reviewing available evidence. A suitable working formulation is:

> In a selected Ghanaian pilot area, relevant environmental observations and historical information may be distributed across different sources. EcoWatch will test whether a traceable, shared workflow can organise those observations, reconstruct change over time, support investigation and prioritisation, and track whether selected interventions produce measurable environmental improvement.

## 10. Evidence gaps to resolve

- Which location has sufficient historical and current data?
- Which environmental pressure is sufficiently documented?
- Which indicators can be measured consistently?
- Which local experts can review interpretations?
- Which communities and institutions should be consulted?
- What data can legally and practically be accessed?
- What reference locations are scientifically suitable?
