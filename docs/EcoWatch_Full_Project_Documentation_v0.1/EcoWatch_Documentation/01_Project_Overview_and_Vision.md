# 01 — EcoWatch Project Overview and Vision

**Status:** Working project definition; foundational direction confirmed, detailed scope still being refined.

## 1. Executive overview

EcoWatch is a proposed environmental intelligence and response system designed to help people and organisations collect environmental evidence, understand changes over time, decide which problems require attention, coordinate interventions and verify whether those interventions improve environmental conditions.

EcoWatch is not intended to be merely a reporting website, a map of complaints, a chatbot or a dashboard that displays disconnected measurements. Its central purpose is to connect environmental observations with analysis, decisions, action and evidence of recovery.

The system is being developed with Ghanaian environmental challenges in mind. Land disturbance and water pollution are important starting concerns, including impacts associated with activities such as illegal mining, but the system's conceptual model recognises that environmental changes can affect vegetation, habitats, biodiversity, farming, fisheries, communities and local economies.

## 2. The problem EcoWatch is trying to address

Environmental problems are often observed by different people and organisations using different methods. A resident may photograph a discoloured river. A farmer may notice changes affecting irrigation. A researcher may collect water-quality measurements. A satellite image may show land-cover change. An environmental officer may document a site visit.

Each item may be useful, but when records are separated by organisation, format, date or location, it can be difficult to reconstruct what happened, identify patterns, understand how impacts are connected, and determine whether a response worked.

EcoWatch proposes a shared evidence and workflow model that can organise these different observations without pretending they all have equal reliability or prove the same thing.

## 3. Vision

A practical, evidence-led environmental intelligence system that helps communities and responsible organisations understand environmental change and support measurable recovery.

## 4. Mission

To make environmental observations more useful by preserving their provenance, connecting relevant evidence, analysing changes and relationships, supporting accountable decisions, and tracking outcomes over time.

## 5. Foundational five-stage process

The five stages are the backbone of EcoWatch:

### 5.1 Collect
Gather information from automated and human sources. Examples include satellite imagery, approved drone imagery, environmental sensors, weather data, scientific records, institutional reports and community submissions.

**Why it matters:** Decisions are only as useful as the evidence available to support them. Collection must include source, time, location and reliability information, not merely a file upload.

### 5.2 Analyse
Compare observations with historical baselines, identify unusual changes, examine trends and assess possible relationships between environmental indicators.

**Why it matters:** A single report rarely explains the full situation. Analysis can reveal patterns that are difficult to notice when records are reviewed separately.

### 5.3 Prioritise
Help responsible people decide which locations or cases need investigation or response first.

**Why it matters:** Resources are limited. Prioritisation should consider severity, exposure, vulnerability, evidence quality, urgency and feasibility—not simply the number of reports.

### 5.4 Intervene
Record and support appropriate investigation, mitigation, enforcement referral where authorised, restoration or other response activities.

**Why it matters:** Monitoring without a route to action can become a record-keeping exercise. EcoWatch should help make responsibility, planned actions and progress visible.

### 5.5 Verify
Compare conditions before and after an intervention and determine whether the intended environmental outcome is supported by evidence.

**Why it matters:** An activity being completed does not prove that the environment recovered. Verification must measure outcomes, acknowledge uncertainty and identify when more work is needed.

The stages form a connected cycle. Verification may reveal new issues and create new collection or analysis needs.

## 6. Guiding principles

1. **Evidence before conclusions:** A report is not automatically a verified incident.
2. **Connected environmental thinking:** Consider relevant links among land, water, ecosystems and human livelihoods.
3. **Traceability:** Preserve the source and history of every important observation and decision.
4. **Uncertainty is visible:** Record unknown, approximate, estimated and disputed information honestly.
5. **Human accountability:** AI may assist analysis but must not independently make consequential enforcement or intervention decisions.
6. **Community participation with protection:** Make reporting accessible while protecting contributors and sensitive information.
7. **Measurable outcomes:** Track environmental conditions, not just activities completed.
8. **Practical growth:** Start with a manageable pilot and expand only when evidence, capacity and resources justify it.
9. **Interoperability:** Prefer documented formats and interfaces that do not unnecessarily lock the project into one provider.
10. **Long-term usefulness:** Design for maintenance, governance and operational ownership, not only a competition demonstration.

## 7. Intended users and stakeholders

Potential user groups include:
- Community members and local observers.
- Farmers, fishers and other people whose livelihoods depend on environmental conditions.
- Environmental researchers and academic institutions.
- Environmental officers and relevant public agencies.
- Civil-society and conservation organisations.
- Restoration teams and project managers.
- System administrators and data stewards.

These are intended stakeholder categories, not confirmed partners or users. Engagement and role permissions must be validated before the pilot.

## 8. What success means

EcoWatch should eventually demonstrate that it can:
- Preserve reliable observations from different sources.
- Reconstruct a useful timeline of environmental change.
- Distinguish reported claims from corroborated or field-verified findings.
- Help identify priority cases using transparent criteria.
- Track response actions and responsible parties.
- Compare environmental indicators before and after intervention.
- Explain what is known, what is uncertain and what evidence is still missing.

A large number of uploads, AI predictions or dashboard charts is not, by itself, proof of success.

## 9. Long-term direction

The first pilot should test a small, meaningful use case. If the approach proves useful, EcoWatch may expand to additional watersheds, environmental pressures, indicators, agencies and data sources. Expansion should follow demonstrated operational and scientific value, not ambition alone.
